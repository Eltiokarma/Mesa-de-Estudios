(1) Our school will hold an earthquake drill next Thursday at 10:00 a.m. (2) Arequipa is in an area where earthquakes are common, so everyone must know what to do.

(3) When the first alarm sounds, students must stop what they are doing and remain calm. (4) Students in a classroom should go under their desks and protect their heads with their arms. (5) When the second alarm sounds, the teachers will lead each class to the yard through the marked exits. (6) Please do not run, and do not go back for your backpack. (7) Students who arrive late will wait with the school secretary until the drill ends.

(8) The drill will last about twenty minutes. (9) Parents are kindly asked not to come to the school during this time, as the entrance must stay free. (10) We also invite families to talk about these steps at home. (11) Thank you for your cooperation.

# t165: Catching Water from the Fog

(1) Lima is a big city on the desert coast, where it almost never rains. (2) Even so, in winter a thick fog covers the hills around the city for several months. (3) For the families who live there, this gray cloud is not just a nuisance; it is a possible source of water. (4) Many of these families do not have water pipes in their homes and must buy water from tank trucks, which is expensive.

(5) A fog net is a simple tool that helps to catch that water. (6) It is a large plastic mesh stretched between two tall posts. (7) As the wind pushes the fog through the mesh, tiny drops stick to the threads and join into bigger drops. (8) These drops fall into a gutter at the bottom of the net, and a pipe carries the water to a tank.

(9) Some communities use the water to grow plants on the dry slopes. (10) Fog water is not a complete solution, because the nets work only when there is fog and the water must be treated before people drink it. (11) Even so, fog nets show that a community can get more from the weather it already has.

# t167: Look, but Do Not Touch

(1) Every year, many visitors travel to the Amazon hoping to swim next to a pink river dolphin. (2) The photos look magical: a smiling tourist, a curious animal and a river shining in the afternoon light. (3) However, I believe that tourists should not be allowed to swim with wild river dolphins.

(4) First, these animals are not pets, and they are not used to crowds. (5) When boats and swimmers come close every day, the dolphins may become stressed, and some may leave the places where they feed or rest. (6) Second, the pink river dolphin is considered an endangered species, so every disturbance matters more.

(7) Some tour operators reply that these visits give local families a reason to protect the river. (8) That argument deserves respect, since many communities need an income. (9) Yet there are other ways to earn it: guided boat trips, in which visitors watch the dolphins from a distance, bring money without forcing any contact. (10) In my view, seeing a dolphin from a quiet boat is a better memory than touching it, and it leaves the animal free. (11) If we protect the dolphins today, the next visitors will be able to enjoy them too.

# t168: Qhapaq Ñan

(1) The Qhapaq Ñan, or Great Inca Road, was a network of roads that connected the many regions of the Inca Empire. (2) In total, it stretched for more than 30,000 kilometers across mountains, deserts and forests. (3) The Incas did not create all of these routes from nothing; they joined older paths built by earlier Andean peoples and improved them.

(4) Where the land was steep, builders made stone stairs, and where rivers were deep, they built bridges of rope. (5) Along the roads there were tambos, buildings where travelers could rest and where food and goods were kept. (6) Because the Incas did not use wheeled vehicles, people traveled on foot, and llamas carried the loads. (7) The roads were also used by the chasquis, runners who passed messages from one post to the next, so news could travel quickly across the empire. (8) Thus, the road system allowed the state to move its armies, collect goods and keep control of distant lands.

(9) In 2014, UNESCO recognized the Qhapaq Ñan as a World Heritage Site, shared by six countries, including Peru. (10) Some stretches can still be walked today.
